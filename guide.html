Coffee Guide

Everything you need to know about coffee — from brewing methods to tasting notes — curated for beginners and enthusiasts alike.

Brewing Methods

Pour Over
Beginner Friendly

Hot water is poured slowly over ground coffee in a filter. The result is a clean, bright cup that highlights the coffee's natural flavors. Great for single-origin beans.

Espresso
Café Classic

Pressurized hot water forces through finely ground coffee in seconds. Produces a concentrated, rich shot with a golden crema on top. The foundation of lattes and cappuccinos.

French Press
Full Body

Coarse ground coffee steeps in hot water for 4 minutes before a plunger filters it. Produces a heavy, full-bodied cup with rich oils intact. Perfect for dark roasts.

Cold Brew
Smooth & Low Acid

Coffee grounds steep in cold water for 12–24 hours. The slow extraction creates a naturally sweet, smooth concentrate. Less acidic and more caffeinated than hot brew.

AeroPress
Versatile

A portable brewer that uses air pressure to push water through coffee quickly. Produces a clean, espresso-like cup. Beloved by travelers and competition baristas alike.

Moka Pot
Italian Classic

Stovetop brewer that uses steam pressure to push water through coffee. Produces a strong, bold brew similar to espresso. A staple in Italian households for decades.

Tasting Tips

🌡️
Water Temperature Matters

Use water between 90–96°C (195–205°F). Boiling water burns the coffee and creates bitterness. Too cool and you'll under-extract, resulting in a sour, weak cup.

⚖️
Use the Right Ratio

A good starting point is 1:15 — 1 gram of coffee for every 15 grams of water. For a stronger cup try 1:12, for a lighter cup 1:17. Adjust to your taste.

🫙
Store Coffee Properly

Keep coffee in an airtight container away from light, heat, and moisture. Avoid the fridge — condensation ruins the beans. Consume within 2–4 weeks of the roast date.

⏱️
Grind Fresh Every Time

Coffee starts losing flavor within minutes of grinding. A burr grinder gives you consistent particle size. Grind right before brewing for the freshest, most vibrant cup.

Frequently Asked Questions

Arabica beans (Coffea arabica) are grown at higher altitudes and have a softer, sweeter taste with hints of fruit and sugar. They contain less caffeine. Robusta beans (Coffea canephora) are heartier, more bitter, and higher in caffeine. Most specialty coffee uses 100% Arabica, while Robusta is commonly found in instant coffee and some espresso blends for extra crema.
Check the roast date on the bag — not the "best by" date. Freshly roasted coffee should be used within 2–4 weeks of the roast date. When you pour hot water over fresh grounds, you'll see them "bloom" — they puff up as CO₂ releases. If there's no bloom, the coffee is stale. Fresh coffee also has a strong, pleasant aroma.
Single origin coffee comes from one specific country, region, farm, or even a single lot. This allows you to taste the unique terroir — the soil, climate, and altitude — of that particular place. Single origin coffees are typically used for pour over or filter methods to highlight their distinct flavors, rather than being blended with other beans.
Bitterness usually means over-extraction — too much flavor was pulled from the grounds. This can happen if your grind is too fine, your water is too hot, or your brew time is too long. Try a coarser grind, lower your water temperature slightly (aim for 93°C), or reduce your brew time. Using too much coffee can also contribute to bitterness.
For beginners, a medium roast from a familiar region like Colombia or Ethiopia is a great start. These tend to be balanced with mild acidity and pleasant sweetness. A simple pour over or French press is the easiest brewing method to learn. As your palate develops, experiment with different origins, roast levels, and brewing styles.
